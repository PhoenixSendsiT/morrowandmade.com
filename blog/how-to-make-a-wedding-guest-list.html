Guest list guide

How to make a wedding guest list (without the family fight)

The guest list sets the size of almost every bill, from catering to chairs. It is also where families tend to disagree. Here is an order that keeps both under control: a number first, then a split, then the names.

Morrow & MadeUpdated Oct 24, 20267 minute read

The short version

  • Set a guest count range from your budget before anyone writes a single name.
  • Agree on the split (couple, each family) before the lists start. There is no rule that it has to be equal.
  • Each of you writes a list separately, in tiers: must invite, would like, only if there's room.
  • Decide plus-ones and kids as rules, not case by case.
  • Expect roughly 75 to 80% of invited guests to come, according to The Knot.

Start with a number, not names

A wedding planner interviewed by The Knot put it simply: guest list, budget and location should be decided together, because guest count is one of the biggest drivers of the total. Every guest adds a meal, a drink package, a chair, a place setting and a slice of cake.

So start from your budget range and work backward to a guest range, for example 90 to 110. Our cost per guest guide shows how to do that math with your own quotes.

Agree on the split before anyone writes names

One planner in The Knot's guide suggests treating the list as a pie, with each family getting a slice. Agreeing on the slices first stops the back-and-forth over every single name.

The traditional split, and the one you actually choose

When both sets of parents contribute, the traditional starting point is half for the couple and a quarter for each family. It is only a starting point.

Source: The Knot, How to Make a Wedding Guest List ("Traditionally, when both sets of parents contribute..."). The Knot also notes there is no rule that the list has to be divided equally.

Couples in planning threads describe other splits that worked for them, such as 80% for the couple and 10% for each family when the couple is paying most of the cost. What matters is agreeing on it early and writing it down.

If a family is contributing money, talk about the guest slice in the same conversation as the money. A contribution with an extra 20 guests attached can cost more than it covers.

Write the lists separately, in tiers

A planner quoted by The Knot has each partner write a separate list, then compare them and look at the overlap. Tiers make the comparison easy:

  1. Must invite.Immediate family, the wedding party, the people you would be hurt not to see there.
  2. Would like to invite.Close friends, extended family you see often, a few colleagues.
  3. Only if there's room.This is your B list. It is where cuts come from, and where invites go if early RSVPs leave space.

Count households as well as people. Invitations go to households, so it is the number you need for stamps and addresses, while people is the number you need for catering.

Decide plus-ones and kids as rules

The Knot's advice is to decide early how you will handle plus-ones, so you are not ruling on each person one at a time. The same goes for children. Pick a rule, apply it to everyone, and the awkward conversations get much shorter.

Rule you could chooseWhat it means
Plus-ones for couples onlyMarried, engaged and living-together couples are invited together. Single guests come solo.
Plus-ones for the wedding partyEveryone standing up with you can bring a guest.
No plus-onesOnly the names on the invitation. Simple, and it can shrink the list a lot.
Immediate-family kids onlyNieces, nephews and the wedding party's children, nobody else's.
Adults-onlySay it on the invitation or wedding website, and say it kindly.

Examples, not etiquette rules. The point is to pick one and apply it to everyone.

How many will actually come?

Not everyone you invite will be able to come. The Knot's rule of thumb is that 75 to 80% of invited guests attend, and its RSVP guidance suggests starting from about 20% declining, then adjusting for travel and timing.

Plan the venue for the full invited number, because everyone might say yes. Plan the food for the RSVPs. And if you are using a B list, The Knot suggests sending invitations at least four months out so there is time to invite B list guests gracefully as declines come in.

How to cut the list when it is too long

  • Cut from the bottom tier first. Never from someone else's must-invites.
  • Apply your rules evenly. If plus-ones go, they go for everyone outside the rule.
  • Try a "last two years" test. A common one: leave off anyone neither of you has spoken to in a couple of years.
  • Look at the money, together. If the list is 30 over the range, put the per-guest cost next to it. Seeing the number usually ends the debate faster than arguing about names.

Keep the list in one place

Most couples collect RSVPs through a wedding website, and that works well. The trouble starts when the guest list lives in one place and the budget lives in another, so a growing list never shows up in the catering line until the final count. Whatever you use, keep households, RSVP status, meal choice and the running headcount together, and connect the headcount to your per-guest costs.

Common questions

Who decides the wedding guest list?

The couple, first. Agree on the total and the split between you and each family before anyone writes names, then each side builds its list within its share.

Does the guest list have to be split equally between families?

No. The traditional split when both sets of parents contribute is half for the couple and a quarter for each family, but The Knot notes there is no rule that it has to be equal. Pick the split that fits who is paying and who you want there.

What percentage of invited guests attend a wedding?

The Knot's rule of thumb is 75 to 80% of invited guests. Start there and adjust for how many guests are traveling and when the wedding is.

When should you send invitations if you have a B list?

At least four months before the wedding, according to a planner in The Knot's guide, so there is time to invite B list guests as declines come in.
