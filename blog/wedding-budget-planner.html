Wedding budget guide

A wedding budget planner needs four numbers, not one.

A single budget total hides the questions that matter: what you expect to spend, what you have signed for, what you have paid, and what you still owe. Keep those four apart and the gaps tell you what to do next. Here is how, with a filled fictional example.

Morrow & MadeUpdated Oct 24, 20268 minute read

The short version

  • Track four numbers: estimated (your plan), committed (signed contracts), paid (money out) and still to pay (committed minus paid).
  • Paid plus still to pay should equal committed. If it does not, a payment is missing from your list.
  • In our fictional example, a $29,920 estimate has $25,030 committed, $13,275 paid and $11,755 still to pay.
  • Estimates are guesses. The gap between estimate and committed is the information, so do not overwrite the estimate when you sign.

The four numbers

Most budget templates have a column for what you planned and a column for what you spent. That leaves a hole in the middle: money you have promised but not yet paid. A deposit is paid, the balance is committed, and the line you have not booked yet is only an estimate. Each is a different kind of knowing.

Why one number is not enough

Couples in public wedding forums describe the same few problems, and they all come from blurring the four numbers together.

Individual couples in public forum threads (Weddingbee, WeddingWire), read Oct 11, 2026. Anecdotal, not a survey.

For scale, a LendingTree survey of 1,050 US newlyweds, fielded in March 2025, found that about two in three (67%) took on debt to pay for the wedding. It is one survey of one group, but it is the reason to see the full picture before the final payments arrive.

The pattern is steady. Small spending slips out of the total, the real number shows up late, and the final balances land in the same few weeks. Our guide to when vendor payments are due shows how much of the money can fall in the last two months.

A quote is not a commitment, and it is still a warning.

The videographer in the example quoted $2,100 against a $1,200 estimate. It is not booked, so it is not in the committed total. But it sits in the vendor list, and the $4,890 not yet committed does not cover it. Seeing that gap before you sign is the point of keeping estimated and committed apart.

Estimates are guesses, and it shows. In the example, 7 booked categories came in above their estimate (by $1,000 in total) and 7 came in below (by $820). Neither is a mistake. The estimate was a plan, and the contract is the fact.

Notice also the two lines at the bottom of the estimate that most budgets forget: tax and service charges ($1,600) and tips and gratuities ($450). Couples in the threads we read name tips and hidden extras more than any other line they wish they had budgeted.

Set it up in five steps

  1. List your categories.Venue, catering, bar, cake, photography, flowers, music, attire, rings, stationery, transportation, rentals and the rest. Add tax and service charges, tips, and a contingency line now, not later. Our free First 30 Days guide lists what goes on top of a quote.
  2. Write an estimate for each one.For per-guest items, enter the price for one guest and multiply by your expected headcount. Fixed items get a flat number. Our guide to cost by guest count shows the formula.
  3. When you sign, record the contract total.Put it in a separate column. Leave the estimate alone, so the gap between the two stays visible.
  4. Give every payment its own row.A deposit, an installment and a final balance are three rows, each with a due date. Leave the paid date empty until you pay.
  5. Check the sum once a month.Paid plus still to pay should equal committed. If it does not, a payment is missing.

How to read the gaps

  • Estimate minus committed. Positive means room left in that category, negative means it came in over. Add the positive and negative gaps across categories to see whether the plan is holding.
  • Committed minus paid. This is the money you still owe on signed contracts. Compare it with the cash you have set aside.
  • Not yet committed. Categories with no contract are the ones where a quote can still surprise you. Get those quotes early.
  • Still to pay by month. The total matters less than when it is due. The payment calendar in our payments guide shows why the last month is usually the heaviest.

Common questions

What is the difference between estimated and committed?

Estimated is your plan: what you expect to spend in each category before you have signed anything. Committed is what you have actually agreed to pay, which is the contract total of each vendor you have booked. A quote is not committed until you sign.

What should a wedding budget include?

The big vendor categories, but also the lines that get forgotten: tax and service charges on catering and venue, tips, attire alterations, postage, and a contingency line. Couples in planning forums mention tips and hidden extras most often as the lines they wish they had budgeted for.

How do I set a per-guest cost in a budget?

List the costs priced per person (catering, bar, cake, rentals), write down the price for one guest, and multiply by your expected headcount. The fixed costs go in a separate column. Our guide to cost by guest count walks through it.

Should I track small purchases?

Couples disagree. Some write down every purchase and keep every receipt. Others only track big items and do not count the little things. If you skip small items, a contingency line gives them somewhere to land.

How much should I keep for contingency?

There is no standard. Our fictional example keeps $800, about 3% of the $29,920 estimate, as its own line. Pick a figure you are comfortable with and keep it visible so it does not quietly become spending money.

Can I use a spreadsheet instead of an app?

Yes. Excel dominates in the planning threads we read, including for couples who use apps for everyday money. What matters is that the sheet keeps the four numbers apart and that every payment has its own row.
